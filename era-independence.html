Independence & the Collapse

July 1, 1960 — October 21, 1969 — January 22, 1991

On June 26, 1960, British Somaliland declared independence. Four days later it merged with Italian Somalia to form the Somali Republic. In thirty-one years it would dissolve into fragments. The middle years held genuine hopes for a unified, democratic nation. The later years held dictatorship, ethnic favoritism, and the machinery of collapse.

The Early Years · 1960–1969

Independence & Democracy

The Somali Republic was born from a union that almost no one expected to work. British Somaliland, administered as a protectorate since 1887, had developed different educational systems, different military structures, and a somewhat different social fabric from Italian Somalia, which had been a direct colony with a different approach to governance. The two regions had different laws, different languages of instruction, and in some cases different memories of what it meant to be Somali.

When independence came, the merger happened peacefully. Aden Abdullah Osman Daar, a businessman from the Somali north, became the first president. Abdirashid Ali Shermarke, a lawyer from the south, became the first prime minister. The constitution enshrined parliamentary democracy. Somali became the official language — the first time a Somali state had unified under a single written language.1I.M. Lewis, A Modern History of the Somali, 4th ed. (Ohio University Press, 2002), pp. 145-160. By 1964, Somalia had held multiple parliamentary elections. Parties competed. Governments fell and were replaced.

The early republic looked toward Ethiopia with a mixed vision: as a former adversary, as a neighbor, and as a fellow postcolonial state. The Somali government pursued irredentist claims to the Ogaden region of Ethiopia and the Northern Frontier District of Kenya — areas with large Somali populations. This was not a fringe position; it had broad support. But it also meant Somalia was building its foreign policy on a boundary dispute with its largest neighbor.

"Somalia is a country where peace is the exception and blood feuds are the rule. Yet for a moment it had democracy anyway."

Ioan M. Lewis, A Modern History of the Somali (2002)

By the mid-1960s, the democratic experiment was showing strain. The number of parties fragmented. Votes became tied to clan loyalty. Voting coalitions had to redistribute power to keep governments stable.4David D. Laitin & Said S. Samatar, Somalia: Nation in Search of a State (Westview Press, 1987), pp. 67–89. Analyzes clan dynamics in parliamentary coalitions and the fragmentation of party politics. This was the machinery of democratic politics everywhere — but in a context where clan identities remained primary, it meant the state was increasingly seen as an instrument of particular clans rather than a national entity. The vision of a unified Somali state was fracturing from within.

The Coup · October 21, 1969

Siad Barre & Military Rule

On October 21, 1969, the government was overthrown in a bloodless coup. Major General Siad Barre, a military officer from the Marehan clan, seized power and declared that the army would rule as trustees of the nation.2Ahmed Ismail Samatar, Socialist Somalia: Rhetoric and Reality (Zed Books, 1988), pp. 12-35. He announced the military would dissolve party politics, eliminate clan favoritism, and build a "scientific socialist" state aligned with the Soviet Union.

Barre was a talented administrator with a vision of state power. He launched literacy campaigns. He reorganized the military. He adopted a Latin alphabet for the Somali language — radically modernizing the written form overnight. He established the Supreme Revolutionary Council as the governing body, with himself as chairman. The early years of his rule were characterized by genuine state capacity and relative order. For five years, many Somalis believed this was exactly what the country needed.

But the foundation was rotten. Barre's revolution claimed to be "scientific socialism" while practicing systematic clan favoritism. The Marehan clan and its allied clans — the "MOD" (Marehan, Ogaden, Dulbahante) — received preferential access to positions in the military, the state security apparatus, and the government.3Abdi Ismail Samatar, The State and Rural Transformation in Northern Somalia, 1884-1986 (University of Wisconsin Press, 1989), pp. 89-112. Barre rotated out military officers from other clans before they could build independent power bases. Clan identity, which the revolution supposedly transcended, became the hidden operating system of the state.

The Ogaden War · 1977–1978

Victory, Then Defeat, Then Collapse

In 1977, Barre made the strategic calculation that would begin the unraveling. He ordered the military into the Ogaden region of Ethiopia, seeking to seize the territory that Somalia claimed as its own.5Saadia Touval, Somali Nationalism: International Politics and the Drive for Unity in the Horn of Africa (Harvard University Press, 1963), pp. 189–201. Touval examines the irredentist ideology that drove Somali claims to the Ogaden and the geopolitical miscalculations of 1977. The invasion was initially successful. The Somali army, trained and equipped by the Soviet Union, drove deep into Ethiopian territory.

But Ethiopia had something Somalia did not: the strategic attention of a superpower that feared losing influence. When the invasion began, Soviet General Vasily Petrov was in Moscow. When Barre looked for Soviet support for his conquest, he found the Soviets had switched sides. The Soviet Union — the same power that had armed and trained Somalia — airlifted Cuban troops to Ethiopia and switched their backing to Mengistu Haile Mariam's military dictatorship there. Within months, the Somali invasion had been reversed. By 1978, Somalia had been defeated.

The consequences cascaded. Hundreds of thousands of ethnic Somali refugees fled Ethiopia into Somalia, straining resources. Barre's legitimacy took a permanent hit. The military, which he had relied on to rule, was humiliated. Most crucially: the army that returned from defeat was angry, demoralized, and increasingly resentful of clan favoritism in promotions and resources. Barre responded to discontent by strengthening the secret police and the clan-based military units he could trust. This meant further consolidation of power in the hands of a narrower group.

The Descent · 1978–1988

Dictatorship & Ethnic Cleansing

After the Ogaden defeat, Barre abandoned the pretense of egalitarianism. The security apparatus expanded. The National Security Service (NSS) became feared throughout the country. Torture became routine in detention facilities. Dissent was not debated — it was eliminated. Military officers from clans outside the MOD coalition were purged or demoted. Resources were funneled to Marehan-dominated units.

The Isaaq clan, from the northern region, began organizing armed resistance. The Somali National Movement (SNM) launched attacks against military installations. Barre's response was not military counter-insurgency — it was ethnic cleansing. In 1988, Barre ordered the air force to bomb Hargeisa, the capital of the northern region. For five days the city was bombarded. Thousands were killed. Thousands more fled to Ethiopia as refugees. Entire neighborhoods were leveled.

The assault on Hargeisa in 1988 was systematic ethnic violence. Soldiers went house to house in Mogadishu, the capital, separating Isaaq civilians and executing them. Whole neighborhoods were cleared. People were dumped in mass graves.6Abdi Ismail Samatar, Africa's First Democrats: Somalia's Stop on Democracy's Long and Arduous Road (Indiana University Press, 2016), pp. 112–128. Samatar documents the state's targeting of the Isaaq clan and the dismantling of democratic institutions. The military did not hide what it was doing — it was sending a message: the state would destroy any clan that resisted.

Other opposition groups emerged. The Somali Patriotic Movement (SPM) fought in the south. The United Somali Congress (USC), based among Hawiye clans in central Somalia, launched attacks in Mogadishu. Barre responded with the same logic: collective punishment of entire communities. But now the state was fracturing into territorial fragments where no single group could establish authority. Government soldiers controlled Mogadishu's center. Opposition forces controlled neighborhoods. By 1989, Mogadishu had become a city of checkpoints and tribal militias.

Barre's state was hollow. It controlled territory through fear, but fear cannot sustain a state when the economy is collapsing, when foreign aid is drying up, and when every clan has organized an armed faction. The military was still the largest organized force in the country — but it was now primarily an instrument of the MOD coalition against everyone else. It was not an army of the nation. It was the military wing of a clan.

The Collapse · 1991

The State Ceases to Exist

By January 1991, the end was visible. The USC had driven deep into Mogadishu. Government forces were in full retreat. Barre tried to regroup south of the capital but lacked the military capacity. On January 26, 1991 — just eight months after the fall of the Berlin Wall — Siad Barre fled Somalia on an airplane, heading to Kenya. Days later, the USC rolled into Mogadishu unopposed.

But the USC's military victory was not a state victory. The various militias that had fought Barre — each organized around clan identity, each armed, each with no unified command structure — could not agree on what came next. The USC wanted to select a president. The Somali National Movement, which had liberated the north, wanted to negotiate as equals. The Somali Patriotic Movement wanted recognition. And behind them were subclans, smaller identity groups, and military commanders who had accumulated power fighting a war and saw no reason to surrender it peacefully.

There was no attempt at a transition government. There was no negotiation. What happened instead was the most complete state collapse in recent African history. The institutions of the Somali state — the parliament, the executive branch, the courts, the civil service — simply ceased to function. The armed forces disbanded. There was no orderly transfer of power because there was no state to transfer. Somalia did not become another authoritarian regime. It became stateless.

By mid-1991, the capital was ruled by competing militias. The country had fractured into clan-controlled zones. There was no government, no army, no police. This was not a failed coup or a civil war that displaced the government while maintaining state structures. This was the dissolution of the state itself. In the capital, they called it "the collapse." In international circles, they called it "state failure." In the Horn of Africa, it was understood as the completion of a process that had been underway since the Ogaden War: the Somali state had proven itself incapable of being a multiclan polity and was decomposing into its constituent parts.

Key Figures

Aden Abdullah Osman Daar
First President (1960–1967)
Businessman from the north who presided over Somalia's early democratic period. Represented the hope that a unified Somali state could function through consensus.
Abdirashid Ali Shermarke
PM & Second President (1964–1969)
Lawyer and political figure. Assassinated on October 15, 1969 by a member of his own guard. His death triggered Barre's coup just days later.
Siad Barre
Military ruler (1969–1991)
Major General who seized power claiming to eliminate clan politics and establish scientific socialism. Instead consolidated power in his clan, ordered the bombing of Hargeisa, and fled the country in 1991.
Mengistu Haile Mariam
Ethiopian dictator
Military ruler of Ethiopia. Switched Soviet support from Somalia to Ethiopia during the Ogaden War. Sent Cuban troops that defeated Somali invasion and humiliated Barre's regime.
