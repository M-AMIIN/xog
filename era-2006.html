The Ethiopian Invasion
& the Birth of al-Shabaab

December 24, 2006 — The Islamic Courts Defeated

For six months in 2006, the Islamic Courts Union controlled Mogadishu and much of central Somalia. They defeated the warlords and brought the first sustained peace in fifteen years. Then Ethiopia invaded, backed by the United States. The invasion destroyed the Courts, radicalized a generation, and created the exact threat it claimed to prevent.

The Courts & the Peace · 2004–2006

Fifteen Years of War Ended by Judges

After the collapse of 1991, Somalia's capital descended into warlordism. Mogadishu was controlled by competing clan militias — the USC, the SPM, and various splinter groups, each with armed wings and territorial ambitions. They fought over neighborhoods. They fought over resources. The capital became a graveyard of security checkpoints where you could be shot depending on which clan controlled which corner. For fifteen years, from 1991 to 2006, there was no government, no police, no judiciary. There was no authority that could enforce any rule other than the threat of violence.

In this vacuum, Somali judges — trained in Islamic law, familiar to their communities — began to organize. They established sharia courts that resolved disputes the traditional way: through Islamic jurisprudence. A merchant with a grievance could bring his case to a court. A clan with a property dispute could seek judgment.4Bronwyn Bruton, Somalia: A New Approach (Council on Foreign Relations, 2010), pp. 18–27. Bruton traces the origins of the Islamic Courts Union from local dispute-resolution mechanisms to a nascent political force. The courts had no government backing and no standing army initially. They had legitimacy born from the fact that they actually resolved disputes instead of perpetuating them.

Over time, as the courts gained legitimacy, some of them organized militias to enforce their decisions. By the early 2000s, a coalition of these courts — eventually called the Islamic Courts Union (ICU) — had begun to challenge the warlords. They defeated militias in neighborhood by neighborhood skirmishes. By June 2006, the ICU had defeated enough warlord factions to control Mogadishu and much of central Somalia.

What happened next was unprecedented in post-1991 Somalia: peace. The ICU imposed order. They disarmed militias. They opened the port that the warlords had closed. Merchants could travel. Women could move through the city. The courts administered justice. There was no Western democracy, no elections, no secular governance. But there was security, and after fifteen years of chaos, security was what Somalis wanted most.

"The Islamic Courts brought peace to Mogadishu for the first time in fifteen years. They did it through Islamic law and the implicit threat of force. It was not liberal democracy. It was order."

International Crisis Group, Somalia: The Tough Part is Ahead (2006)

The international community was not pleased. The ICU was Islamic. Some of its leadership had connections to al-Qaeda operatives. A few of the courts were run by radical clerics. But most of the courts were conservative, focused on applying Islamic law to everyday disputes. The issue was not that the courts were inherently extreme — it was that they were Islamic, and in 2006, any Islamic authority in a strategically important region triggered American counterterrorism protocols.

The Invasion · December 2006

Ethiopia & the United States

Ethiopia's government, led by Prime Minister Meles Zenawi, was deeply concerned by the rise of an Islamic authority in Somalia. Ethiopia is predominantly Christian, and it has a history of conflict with Islamic states. The Ogaden region of Ethiopia has large ethnic Somali and Muslim populations, and Addis Ababa feared that an Islamic Somalia could inspire separatism on Ethiopia's side of the border.5Stig Jarle Hansen, Al-Shabaab in Somalia (Oxford University Press, 2013), pp. 46–68. Hansen details Ethiopia's strategic calculations and the geopolitical concerns driving the intervention.

The United States, under the Bush administration, shared the concern but for different reasons. In the post-9/11 environment, the U.S. was focused on removing any Islamist government that might harbor terrorists. The ICU, even though most of its courts were moderate, included some individuals with ideological ties to al-Qaeda. That was enough. The U.S. decided that the ICU had to go.

What happened next was a classic proxy war dynamic. The United States provided military aid, intelligence support, and diplomatic cover to Ethiopia. Ethiopia mobilized its military. On December 24, 2006 — Christmas Eve in the Western calendar — Ethiopian forces invaded Somalia. They attacked from the northwest, moving toward Mogadishu. The ICU militia fought back but was outgunned by the Ethiopian army.

By January 2007, the ICU had been defeated. The Ethiopians rolled into Mogadishu unopposed. The courts' militias had either fled, disbanded, or melted into the population. The Somali warlords, who had been displaced by the ICU, were now back in positions of power, armed by Ethiopia and dependent on Ethiopian military presence for their authority.

The security the ICU had brought lasted exactly six months.

The Insurgency · 2007 Onward

Creating the Threat You Fight

The Ethiopian occupation triggered an insurgency. Young Somalis who had been part of the ICU courts but had not been al-Qaeda operatives before the invasion suddenly had a reason to fight against a foreign military occupation. Some formed the Somali Resistance Movement (SRM). Some reorganized as militias loyal to the remaining ICU leadership in exile. And some, the most ideologically committed, joined or formed a hardline splinter group called al-Shabaab.

Al-Shabaab emerged from the Youth Wing (Shabab) of the ICU as a hardline splinter committed to jihad against foreign occupation and secular governance.1Stig Jarle Hansen, Al-Shabaab in Somalia (Oxford University Press, 2013), pp. 34-45. Its founding figures included Aden Hashi Ayro, a former military officer trained in Afghanistan, and Omar Shafik Hammami, an American-born jihadist recruiter. Al-Shabaab launched suicide bombings and roadside ambushes against Ethiopian forces and their Somali allies.

The American role was not public, but it was extensive. The U.S. Central Intelligence Agency operated bases in the region. American personnel coordinated with Ethiopian forces. The U.S. conducted drone strikes against suspected al-Qaeda operatives in Somalia — strikes that sometimes hit civilians. The stated goal was counterterrorism. The effect was to provide recruits with a powerful narrative: a foreign occupation backed by a foreign power against Islam itself.

The African Union, under international pressure, sent a peacekeeping mission called AMISOM (African Union Mission in Somalia) in 2007. AMISOM troops, primarily from Uganda and Burundi, were supposed to stabilize the country. Instead, they arrived to find a country in open conflict. They became part of the security infrastructure that opposed the insurgency. This meant they also became targets.

By 2008, al-Shabaab was conducting sophisticated attacks: assassinating government officials, bombing AMISOM barracks, and carrying out suicide bombings across Mogadishu and the countryside.2Cedric Barnes and Harun Hassan, "The Rise and Fall of Mogadishu's Islamic Courts," Journal of Eastern African Studies 1, no. 2 (2007), pp. 151-160. Aden Hashi Ayro was killed in a U.S. drone strike in 2008, but al-Shabaab reorganized under new leadership. By 2009, the group controlled large parts of southern and central Somalia, commanding an insurgency that drew recruits from both Somalia and diaspora communities worldwide.

The 2006 invasion was intended to prevent an Islamist takeover of Somalia. Instead, it created the conditions for al-Shabaab to emerge as a far more radical and violent organization than the Islamic Courts had ever been.3Ken Menkhaus, "The Crisis in Somalia: Tragedy in Five Acts," African Affairs 106, no. 204 (2007), pp. 357-390.

The Outcome · 2012 & Beyond

A Decade of Occupation

The Ethiopian military remained in Somalia for five years. They slowly lost control of rural areas. They faced a growing insurgency. The Somali government, now led by weak administrations dependent on foreign military support, had no popular legitimacy. AMISOM troops remained and remain to this day, still fighting al-Shabaab, still unable to fully defeat them.

By 2012, the Ethiopians withdrew. AMISOM stayed. Al-Shabaab controlled less territory but continued to conduct attacks. A federal government was established in Mogadishu, supported by foreign military power, but it lacked authority in much of the country. Al-Shabaab conducted suicide bombings at government buildings, at AMISOM barracks, at civilian targets. They conducted attacks on hotels. They dug in as an insurgent force, financed by extortion and by relationships with international jihadist networks.

By 2016, with al-Shabaab still active and still strong, Somalia held elections for a new parliament. But the war had not ended. The invasion had not prevented Islamism — it had radicalized it.6Ken Menkhaus, Somalia: State Collapse and the Threat of Terrorism (IISS Adelphi Paper 364, 2004), pp. 53–71. Menkhaus analyzes how counterterrorism operations and civilian casualties created grievances that al-Shabaab exploited for recruitment. The U.S. drone strikes, meant to kill terrorists, had killed civilians and radicalized those who lost family members. The foreign occupation had done exactly what foreign occupations usually do: it had created the enemy it was meant to defeat.

Key Figures

Sheikh Sharif Sheikh Ahmed
ICU Leader & President
Moderate Islamist scholar who led the Islamic Courts Union. Fled to Kenya after the invasion. Later returned to Somalia and served as president 2009–2012, attempting to bridge the divide between the state and Islamic forces.
Meles Zenawi
Ethiopian PM
Authoritarian leader of Ethiopia. Concerned about Islamic authority in Somalia, he mobilized the invasion with full American backing and maintained Ethiopian forces in Somalia for five years.
Aden Hashi Ayro
al-Shabaab Co-founder
Former military officer who trained with jihadist networks in Afghanistan. Emerged as a leader of the Youth Wing of ICU, then co-founded al-Shabaab. Killed in a U.S. drone strike in May 2008.
Omar Shafik Hammami
al-Shabaab Ideologue
American-born Islamist who joined al-Shabaab as a recruiter and propagandist. Represented the internationalization of the insurgency. Killed in 2015 by al-Shabaab rivals.
