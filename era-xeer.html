The Xeer
Somali Customary Law

No police · No courts · No prisons

A man is killed. From that moment his kin hold the right to a life for his life. Elders go between the two sides, and if the dead man's kin accept, the killer's group pays mag, blood compensation, in its place: one hundred camels for a man. That is the xeer, the body of agreements by which Somali groups settle disputes. A life answers a life unless an agreement says otherwise. Colonial codes were set above it, and a military government outlawed its collective payments. The xeer is still in use.

A case under the acacia

Walk one dispute, from the killing to the peace

1 / the harm

A man is killed

A man of one lineage kills a man of another. There is no state to arrest him and no court to summon him. The dead man's kin now hold the right to a life for his life, and if the killer's group shields him, the life they take may be another of its men.

2 / the council

The elders go between

A delegation of elders, the ergo, is sent by one side, by both, or by a neutral group, to keep the dispute from spreading and to prepare a hearing. The judges are elders drawn from both groups and sometimes a third, and no one close to either party may sit.

3 / the choice

Blood or camels

100
camels · the mag of a man

The dead man's kin choose: take the life, accept mag in its place, or forgive. When they accept, the xeer between the two groups sets the amount. One hundred camels for a man and fifty for a woman is the common measure, taken from the sharia, and local agreements can set it lower.

4 / why it holds

What makes them pay

Camels are paid because a life can be taken. If the mag is refused or never paid, the wronged group may kill the killer or a man of his group, and a cycle of revenge killings begins. Between groups of equal standing, a ruling holds through fear of that cycle, pressure from the community, respect for the judges, the strength of each side, and earlier cases settled the same way.

5 / the peace

The group pays together

The killer does not pay alone. His whole mag-paying group carries the debt. About a dozen camels go at once to the dead man's household and two dozen to his closest relatives, and the rest are paid over an agreed time to his wider group. With the mag paid, the claim on a life is settled.

after André Le Sage, Stateless Justice in Somalia, 2005, and Joakim Gundel, The Predicament of the 'Oday', 2006
How Xeer Works

Law Without Lawyers

For a killing, the xeer starts from the right of the dead man's kin. They may take a life for his life, accept mag in its place, or forgive.1Joakim Gundel, The Predicament of the 'Oday' (Danish Refugee Council and Novib/Oxfam, 2006), p. 20, on how killings are settled in Somaliland: "Usually, the victim family is given the right to choose either to kill or receive mag payment or forgive." Mag is the Somali word for blood compensation; diya is the Arabic. The Quran sets the same rule: retaliation for a killing, which the kin of the dead may forgive in return for payment (2:178). The xeer takes its measures from the sharia of the Shafi'i school, as Somali sheikhs assess them.2Gundel, pp. 8 and 10: the sharia entered the xeer mainly through the Shafi'i school, and compensation for injury and homicide is "based on Sharia, and assessed by the sheikhs, traditionally according to the Shafi'ite standards". That law separates a deliberate killing from an accident. Around 2000 the Islamic courts of south Mogadishu punished a proven deliberate murder with death and an accidental killing with one hundred camels.3André Le Sage, Stateless Justice in Somalia (Centre for Humanitarian Dialogue, 2005), p. 45 and note 72. For a shot meant for one man that killed another, the courts took one hundred camels of a higher grade, forty of them pregnant.

Xeer means agreement. Two groups that live and move side by side make one between them, and a man has standing in the xeer only through the group he pays mag with.4Gundel, pp. 6 to 8: xeer "means contract or agreement, usually entered bilaterally between two mag-paying groups", made by elders of clans "that live and migrate adjacent to one another"; only through membership of a mag-paying group does a man have "any political and jural status". Gundel cites I.M. Lewis, A Pastoral Democracy (1961), p. 161. Between groups bound in this way, most killings end in mag.5Gundel, p. 20: "Homicides are usually solved through agreement on mag compensation." Each agreement sets its own amount. One hundred camels for a man and fifty for a woman is the general measure, local agreements can set it lower, and it varies with the size and wealth of the groups.6Gundel, p. 10: the rates are "embodied in the xeer treaties between the different clans", and "Local agreements can establish lower levels of blood compensation." Le Sage, p. 33: the amount "may vary relative to the size and wealth of the clans involved". People who are party to no xeer with their hosts, such as families displaced from the south into Puntland, have no standing in it at all.7Gundel, p. 24: migrants from Ethiopia and displaced people from southern Somalia "do not have a jural status with clans in Puntland, because they are not party to any xeer".

There are no courts. The judges are the xeer beegti, elders chosen for the case from both groups and sometimes from a neutral third. They judge by precedent, held in memory and in well-known proverbs, and where there is none they follow a proverb of their own: "If something happens that we have never seen or heard before, we will make a judgement in a way that has never been seen or heard before."8Le Sage, pp. 34 to 35: precedent has been "orally codified" in well-known proverbs, and the judges are drawn from the aggrieved clan, the offending clan and possibly a neutral clan. Most groups choose mediation, masalaxo, over strict arbitration, gar dawe. The wronged side receives less, and gets a ruling both groups will keep.9Le Sage, p. 35, drawing on the Puntland Development Research Center's study of Somali customary law (2003). A group that rejects a ruling can say "I wish to choose another tree" and have the case heard again, up to three times.10Le Sage, p. 36.

There is no police force. What makes a group pay is the life it could lose. If the mag is refused or never paid, the wronged group may kill the killer or a man of his group, and a cycle of revenge killings begins.11Le Sage, p. 34; Gundel, p. 9. Le Sage defines anno as "revenge killing by one clan or sub-clan against another in the absence of diya payment" (p. 5). Revenge killing, called aano among the Dulbahante and Isaaq and dakano among the Majeerteen, is an old Somali way of forcing a group to pay what it owes.12Gundel, p. 24: "Somali revenge killing is an old Somali cultural practice used to enforce a non-compliant clan to pay compensation." When the killer's group will not hand him over, the revenge falls on the man whose loss will hurt that group most.13ACCORD, Clans in Somalia: Report on a Lecture by Joakim Gundel (2009), p. 21. The xeer has no impartial force behind it. When a strong group refuses a ruling in favour of a weak one, the weak group cannot make it comply, and Somali minority groups, particularly those of Bantu and Arab origin, are heavily discriminated against in its decisions.14Le Sage, p. 36.

The Scale of Compensation

Graduated Justice

Below a life, the xeer grades every harm, and the mag of a man is the measure for the rest.15Le Sage, p. 33: "the benchmark for compensation is the penalty apportioned for wrongly taking someone's life."

Its penal part, the xeer dhiig, has three heads: dil, a killing, answered with mag; qoon, a wound, answered with qoomaal; and dalliil, an insult, answered with haal.16Gundel, p. 10. Physical harm alone runs through some twelve degrees, from torn clothing to broken bones, open wounds and the loss of a limb, each with its own payment.17Le Sage, p. 33. The mag is reckoned in camels and today is usually paid in money at their value.18Gundel, pp. 10 and 20: "in practice most often translated into currency equivalent"; "mag today is valued in terms of Camels, but paid in cash". The same collective payment answers theft, rape and defamation.19Le Sage, p. 32, among the xissi adkaaday, the principles of the xeer held to be immutable and common to all Somali clans. A woman who is raped is often forced to marry her attacker. The practice is defended as protecting her honour, and it also secures full payment of her dowry from his group and ties the two groups together.20Le Sage, p. 38, citing the Puntland Development Research Center's study (2003), pp. 36 to 37.

The amounts move when the elders agree to move them. In parts of Somaliland the mag had been raised until groups found it hard to pay, and in Togdheer the elders formed an organisation of their own, Haqsoor, to stop the revenge killings. In workshops in 2003 and 2004 and a conference in Burao, the aqiil, the lineage elders who collect the mag, signed a new xeer across their clans: killers would be handed to the police, mag would stay at one hundred camels, and all of it would go to the dead man's family. The same resolution affirmed a widow's right to inherit and to marry a man of her choice. The aqiil reported a significant fall in revenge killings.21Gundel, pp. 20 to 21. Haqsoor worked with the advocacy programme of the Danish Refugee Council.

Collective Responsibility

The Group That Pays

The group that pays is the mag-paying group: a few hundred to a few thousand men who trace their descent to a common ancestor four to eight generations back. No man can pay one hundred camels alone, and only as a member of such a group does he have any standing in the xeer.22Gundel, p. 6 and note 5. Inside it, about a third of the mag, the jiffo, passes between the closest kin of the killer and of the dead, and the rest between the two groups as a whole.23Gundel, p. 10, citing Lewis (1961), pp. 172 to 173: the jiffo is usually rated at 33 camels. Le Sage, p. 33, describes the same division: about a dozen camels to the dead man's household and two dozen to his closest relatives at once, and the remaining sixty or so over an agreed time to his wider group. A man who kills puts the debt, and the risk of revenge, on all of them.

Members do resist paying for another man's killing. Disagreement over dividing the mag is the most common reason a group splits, and frequent mag debts can break a group apart.24Gundel, p. 7; Le Sage, pp. 34 to 35, quoting the Puntland Development Research Center: "splinter groups can sometimes break off from a Diya-paying unit as a result of frequent Diya violations". In Somaliland the aqiil can take a man who refuses to pay his share to court.25Gundel, p. 20.

The system also applies to grazing rights, water access, and territorial disputes. If two clans have a dispute over access to a well, the elders of both clans meet and consult precedent. Historically, was the well shared? Who had priority in drought seasons? What arrangements were made in prior generations? The elders draw on this collective memory to issue judgment. The judgment is enforced through the same collective mechanism: acceptance by both clans or escalation until pressure forces acceptance.

Colonial Encounter

Resistance to Replacement

Britain in the north and Italy in the south set their own codes above the xeer, above all for serious crimes, and both left affairs between Somalis to the xeer wherever public order was not at stake. A British order of 1898 recognised that Somalis were bound by customary law, and an Italian law of 1908 recognised them as subject to customary law and the sharia.26Le Sage, pp. 17 to 18: the 1898 Principal Order-in-Council in British Somaliland, and Law No. 161 of 5 April 1908 in Italian Somalia. The colonial courts regulated the xeer and never displaced it.27Le Sage, p. 18: the colonial judicial system "was able to regulate, but not displace, the continued practice of Somali customary justice".

At independence the Somali Republic kept the xeer as a lawful way to settle disputes between clans.28Le Sage, p. 18. In 1968 the government's National Advisory Council, made up of traditional leaders and other knowledgeable people from every region, advised that collective responsibility and diya be abolished, and that personal responsibility, with the death penalty for capital crimes, take their place.29Le Sage, p. 34, quoting the Puntland Development Research Center's study (2003), p. 25.

The coup of 1969 stopped the government from acting on that advice. The military government that took power outlawed collective responsibility instead, and a code of 1973, in the words of a Hargeisa workshop report, made "any homicide offence... punishable by death and compensation payable only to close relatives".30Le Sage, p. 34, gives 1971 for the attempt to outlaw collective responsibility; on p. 20 he dates the law that abolished collective responsibility and diya payment to 1 November 1970 (Law No. 67). The words on the 1973 code come from the Academy for Peace and Development, The Judicial System in Somaliland (Hargeisa, 2002), quoted on p. 19. Strong public resistance and weak enforcement in the nomadic areas forced the government to drop the law in 1974, and from July of that year the settlements reached by elders could be registered with a district court and enforced.31Le Sage, p. 34, quoting the Puntland Development Research Center (2003), p. 29, and Law No. 19 of 27 July 1974. The xeer outlived that government and the collapse of the state in 1991, and it remains the predominant justice system wherever the regional administrations do not reach.32Le Sage, p. 32.

Xeer in Practice

Water, Marriage, Grazing

In pastoral Somalia, xeer continues to regulate the resources that matter most: water, land, marriage, and the conduct of warfare.

Water rights. Wells and boreholes are owned by clans or jointly by clans. Xeer specifies who can draw water, in what season, in what quantity. In drought, the rules change — access is shared more broadly to prevent wells from being monopolized by any single clan. The elders of the well-owning clans meet with elders of surrounding clans and renegotiate temporary access. No written contract exists. The arrangement is enforced through recognition of its legitimacy and through the threat of exclusion or violence if it is violated.

Marriage. Xeer specifies bride-price, defines obligations of husband and wife, and provides remedies for breach of marriage contract. If a man divorces his wife unjustly, he must return the bride-price. If a wife leaves her husband without cause, her family must return it. These are the terms known and accepted by all parties when they enter the agreement.

Grazing and pasture. Xeer divides pasture lands by clan, defines seasonal grazing rights, and mediates disputes when drought forces competition for resources. In good years, the boundaries are clear and enforced. In drought, they become flexible through negotiation among elders, guided by precedent and principle rather than written rules.

Conduct of warfare. When clans do go to war, xeer specifies rules of engagement: non-combatants are protected, wounded are not pursued, women and children are not targeted. These rules are not always obeyed, but they are the standard that communities recognize and enforce through judgment of war leaders who violate them.

Survival and Adaptation

Why Xeer Persists

The Xeer has survived colonialism, attempted state-building, civil war, and the pressures of modernity because it solves a problem that no external system can solve as effectively: how to maintain order in a society without a centralized police force or court system.

The system is not perfect. It depends on broad consensus, which can break down. It favors established power structures — wealthy clan leaders have more influence than poor ones. It can be harsh: expulsion from the clan can mean death in a hostile pastoralist environment.33Le Sage, pp. 36 to 37: a militarily strong clan can refuse a judgment that favours a weak one, and the xeer is weak in the towns, where many of the groups that meet have no bilateral xeer between them. But it works. Communities that rely on xeer maintain internal order, resolve disputes, and manage shared resources without external enforcement.

Modern scholars have noted that xeer functions as a complete legal system: it addresses property rights, contract law, family law, criminal law, and tort. It includes remedies (compensation), enforcement mechanisms (clan pressure), and a system for updating law (elder consensus on new cases). The only thing it lacks is writing. But for societies where oral transmission is reliable and collective memory is enforced by common interest, writing may be less important than theorists assume.

The persistence of xeer into the 21st century, despite state prohibition and external pressure, suggests that legal systems built on consensus and collective benefit are more resilient than those imposed from outside.
