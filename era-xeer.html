The Xeer
Somali Customary Law

No police · No courts · No prisons

A man is killed. His clan demands one hundred camels from the killer's clan. The elders of both sides meet. The genealogies are recited to establish the relationships. The precedents are reviewed. The compensation is paid. No court, no police, no prison, no state. The xeer — Somali customary law — has been settling disputes this way for longer than anyone can date. Colonial powers tried to replace it. The Somali state tried to replace it. Both failed. The xeer is still in use.

A case under the acacia

Walk one dispute, from the killing to the peace

1 / the harm

A man is killed

A man of one clan kills a man of another. There is no state to arrest him and no court to summon him. What stands in their place is the xeer, and the elders who carry it in memory.

2 / the council

The elders convene

Elders of both clans sit together. No king appoints them and no king pays them. They judge from precedent, which they hold by heart, because the xeer was never written down and has not needed to be.

3 / the reckoning

The price of a life

100
camels · the life of a man

The xeer sets a price on every harm, and runs a graduated scale below this one for lesser injuries, each figure as fixed as any written civil code. A death is reckoned at one hundred camels, paid in livestock, not taken in blood.

4 / why it holds

Even the strong pay

In a system of revenge, a powerful clan can kill and fear no answer. Under the xeer, even a powerful clan pays, and one hundred camels is a cost heavy enough that both sides would rather settle than let claim breed counter-claim until the herds are gone.

5 / the peace

The clan pays together

The killer does not pay alone. His whole clan carries the debt, so his whole clan had reason to restrain him before the act and to counsel him after it. The camels change hands, an oath seals the judgment, and the feud is closed before revenge can begin its cycle. No state stood anywhere in this.

How Xeer Works

Law Without Lawyers

Nothing is written down. The core principle is mag — blood compensation, also called diya. A man kills another man: his clan owes the victim's clan one hundred camels.1I.M. Lewis, A Pastoral Democracy (1961), ch. 8, pp. 161–170Detailed account of mag rates and the dia-paying group structure A woman killed: fifty camels. The numbers are not negotiable. They are fixed, known to everyone, graded by the severity of injury from murder down to insult. The scale has been stable for as long as anyone has recorded it.

The logic is economic. In a revenge system, a powerful clan can kill with impunity — who will punish them? In a compensation system, even a powerful clan pays. One hundred camels is a serious cost. It gives every clan an incentive to settle rather than escalate, because escalation means counter-claims, counter-compensation, and eventual depletion of the herd. The system turns justice into a problem both sides have a financial interest in solving.

There are no courts. A dispute goes before clan elders — men whose authority comes from age, reputation, and knowledge of precedent. The elders hear both sides. They consult previous cases held in oral memory: what was decided the last time this kind of dispute arose, and the time before that.2I.M. Lewis, A Pastoral Democracy (1961), ch. 7On the role of elders as arbiters and the oral precedent system They issue a judgment. If both sides accept, the matter is closed. If one side refuses, the dispute escalates — and at that point, neighboring clans intervene, because unresolved disputes threaten everyone's grazing access and trade.

There is no police force. If a judgment is issued and a clan refuses to pay compensation, the remedy is economic or military pressure from the clan of the victim. This creates mutual incentive: the victim's clan has incentive to see the judgment respected; the defendant's clan has incentive to convince the defendant to pay rather than face escalation.

"The Xeer system has operated for centuries without a state, maintaining public order through collective enforcement and mutual interest in keeping disputes from spiraling into endless vendetta."

The Scale of Compensation

Graduated Justice

The Xeer developed a graduated scale of compensation that parallels the specificity of codified civil law. Consider the scale of injury compensation:

Murder: 100 camels (sometimes reckoned as 1,000 shillings in modern cash equivalents). Blinding of one eye: 50 camels. Permanent disability: compensation scaled by severity. Temporary injury: compensation scaled by healing time. Insult to honor: smaller compensation. Theft: return of the stolen item plus additional compensation. Rape: 100 camels plus marriage if the victim wishes. Breach of marriage contract: return of bride-price plus additional compensation.

The system distinguishes between types of harm, grades compensation by severity, and includes remedies for both physical and reputational injury.3Michael van Notten, The Law of the Somalis (Red Sea Press, 2005), pp. 45–68Van Notten's analysis of the xeer compensation schedule, compiled from interviews with Somali elders in the 1990s The system creates incentive for settlement rather than vendetta because the payment is known, fixed, and within the capability of most clans to pay if they must.

Collective Responsibility

How the Clan Enforces Law

The Xeer functions through collective responsibility. If one man of a clan commits murder, the entire clan is responsible for paying the compensation or facing the consequences. This creates powerful incentive for the clan to control its members. A man contemplating violence knows that his action will burden his entire clan with the cost of compensation. His kinsmen will pressure him not to act. His elders will counsel restraint.

If a member refuses to respect the judgment of the elders, the clan has remedies: they can seize his property, exclude him from benefits, or declare him expelled from the clan. Expulsion from the clan is an extreme penalty — it removes a man from the kinship network that provides security, provides water rights, provides marriage alliance. It is functionally equivalent to execution, though without the bloodshed.

The system also applies to grazing rights, water access, and territorial disputes. If two clans have a dispute over access to a well, the elders of both clans meet and consult precedent. Historically, was the well shared? Who had priority in drought seasons? What arrangements were made in prior generations? The elders draw on this collective memory to issue judgment. The judgment is enforced through the same collective mechanism: acceptance by both clans or escalation until pressure forces acceptance.

Colonial Encounter

Resistance to Replacement

When British and Italian colonizers arrived in the 19th century, they attempted to replace xeer with European law codes. They established courts, appointed judges, and declared xeer to be "customary law" — a designation that was intended to marginalize it as less legitimate than the written colonial code.

It persisted in parallel with colonial courts. Disputes brought to colonial courts would be re-litigated in xeer courts. Judgments from the magistrate would be overridden by elder decisions if they conflicted with xeer precedent. The colonial authorities eventually learned to accommodate the system rather than suppress it — partly out of pragmatism (the xeer system worked), partly out of administrative necessity (they could not police the vast pastoralist territories without local cooperation).

Xeer survived colonialism, the Siad Barre government's attempts to replace it with state law in the 1970s, and the collapse of central authority in 1991.4I.M. Lewis, A Modern History of the Somali, 4th ed. (Ohio University Press, 2002), pp. 264–268Lewis documents Siad Barre's 1975 Family Law that attempted to override xeer's marriage and inheritance provisions — the law was widely ignored in rural areas The system persists because the communities that use it find it more legitimate than any alternative imposed from outside.

Xeer in Practice

Water, Marriage, Grazing

In pastoral Somalia, xeer continues to regulate the resources that matter most: water, land, marriage, and the conduct of warfare.

Water rights. Wells and boreholes are owned by clans or jointly by clans. Xeer specifies who can draw water, in what season, in what quantity. In drought, the rules change — access is shared more broadly to prevent wells from being monopolized by any single clan. The elders of the well-owning clans meet with elders of surrounding clans and renegotiate temporary access. No written contract exists. The arrangement is enforced through recognition of its legitimacy and through the threat of exclusion or violence if it is violated.

Marriage. Xeer specifies bride-price, defines obligations of husband and wife, and provides remedies for breach of marriage contract. If a man divorces his wife unjustly, he must return the bride-price. If a wife leaves her husband without cause, her family must return it. These are the terms known and accepted by all parties when they enter the agreement.

Grazing and pasture. Xeer divides pasture lands by clan, defines seasonal grazing rights, and mediates disputes when drought forces competition for resources. In good years, the boundaries are clear and enforced. In drought, they become flexible through negotiation among elders, guided by precedent and principle rather than written rules.

Conduct of warfare. When clans do go to war, xeer specifies rules of engagement: non-combatants are protected, wounded are not pursued, women and children are not targeted. These rules are not always obeyed, but they are the standard that communities recognize and enforce through judgment of war leaders who violate them.

Survival and Adaptation

Why Xeer Persists

The Xeer has survived colonialism, attempted state-building, civil war, and the pressures of modernity because it solves a problem that no external system can solve as effectively: how to maintain order in a society without a centralized police force or court system.

The system is not perfect. It depends on broad consensus, which can break down. It favors established power structures — wealthy clan leaders have more influence than poor ones. It can be harsh: expulsion from the clan can mean death in a hostile pastoralist environment.5André Le Sage, "Stateless Justice in Somalia," Centre for Humanitarian Dialogue (2005), pp. 15–22Le Sage documents both the strengths and limitations of xeer in contemporary southern Somalia, noting its bias toward powerful clans and its difficulty handling inter-clan disputes in urban settings But it works. Communities that rely on xeer maintain internal order, resolve disputes, and manage shared resources without external enforcement.

Modern scholars have noted that xeer functions as a complete legal system: it addresses property rights, contract law, family law, criminal law, and tort. It includes remedies (compensation), enforcement mechanisms (clan pressure), and a system for updating law (elder consensus on new cases). The only thing it lacks is writing. But for societies where oral transmission is reliable and collective memory is enforced by common interest, writing may be less important than theorists assume.

The persistence of xeer into the 21st century, despite state prohibition and external pressure, suggests that legal systems built on consensus and collective benefit are more resilient than those imposed from outside.
