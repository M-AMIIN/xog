The Republic Nobody Recognizes

1991 → PRESENT

In 1991, while the south collapsed into civil war, the former British Somaliland territory declared independence, wrote a constitution, and built a democracy. No country on earth recognizes it.

WORLD / 1991 — While the Soviet Union was dissolving and Yugoslavia was fracturing, a corner of Somalia was building a state that the international community would refuse to acknowledge.
The Declaration

The Declaration

The collapse of Siad Barre's regime in January 1991 plunged Somalia into civil war. In the south, clan-based militias fought for control of Mogadishu and the country's resources. In the north, the Isaaq clans who had suffered the worst of Barre's repression saw an opportunity to escape the chaos.

Between 1988 and 1990, Barre's forces had conducted a scorched-earth campaign against the Isaaq, bombing Hargeisa and Burao and killing an estimated 50,000 to 100,000 people. The Isaaq had every reason to believe that a unified Somalia would mean continued domination by southern clans. On May 18, 1991, the elders of the Somali National Movement declared the independence of the Republic of Somaliland.

The declaration was not simply a reaction to southern chaos. It was based on a legal argument: that British Somaliland had been an independent state for five days in 1960 before entering a voluntary union with Italian Somalia, and that the union had failed. Somaliland, the argument went, was not seceding from Somalia; it was dissolving a failed union — the same legal logic that Eritrea used when it separated from Ethiopia in 1993.

State-Building

Building Without Recognition

Without international recognition — and therefore without access to World Bank loans, IMF programs, or most bilateral aid — Somaliland has built a functioning state.1Mark Bradbury, Becoming Somaliland (Indiana University Press, 2008), p. 145. It has held five peaceful presidential elections, with transfers of power between rival parties. It has maintained its own currency, police force, and military. It has provided basic services to a population of roughly 4 million people.

The 1993 Borama Conference was the foundation.4Marleen Renders, Consider Somaliland: State-Building with Traditional Leaders and Institutions (Brill, 2012), pp. 54–88. Renders provides a detailed account of the Borama Conference's role in establishing Somaliland's peace framework and political institutions. Using traditional xeer principles, clan elders negotiated a peace agreement that ended inter-clan fighting and established a framework for governance. The conference elected Muhammad Haji Ibrahim Egal as president and created a bicameral legislature: an elected lower house and an upper house of clan elders.

The constitution, adopted by referendum in 2001, established a presidential system with a five-year term limit. Since then, Somaliland has held elections in 2003 (decided by 80 votes), 2010, 2017, and 2021. Each transfer of power has been peaceful.2Marleen Renders, Consider Somaliland: State-Building with Traditional Leaders and Institutions (Brill, 2012), pp. 189-192.

Recognition

The Recognition Problem

No country has recognized Somaliland's independence. The African Union, committed to the principle of uti possidetis (maintaining colonial borders), has refused to entertain the question.3I.M. Lewis, A Modern History of the Somali, 4th ed. (Ohio University Press, 2002), pp. 238-240. The United States and European powers, focused on stabilizing the rest of Somalia, have not wanted to complicate matters by recognizing a breakaway region. Ethiopia, Somaliland's most important trading partner and security ally, has refused recognition while maintaining close informal ties.5Africa Watch/Human Rights Watch, Somalia: A Government at War with its Own People (1990). Documents the 1988 bombardment of Hargeisa and the basis for Somaliland's separation from Somalia.

The uti possidetis argument — that African borders should remain as they were at independence — is the primary obstacle to recognition. But Somaliland's supporters point out that the principle is selectively applied. Eritrea was recognized after separating from Ethiopia. South Sudan was recognized after separating from Sudan. The difference, critics say, is that Somaliland is not strategically important enough to justify the precedent.

Somaliland has built a functioning democracy without recognition, while the internationally recognized government of Somalia has struggled to establish authority beyond Mogadishu. The international community's commitment to Somalia's territorial integrity has meant ignoring a successful state-building project in favor of a failed one. Somaliland waits, unrecognized but undeterred.
