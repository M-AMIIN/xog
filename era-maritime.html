The Maritime Networks

Monsoon Trade · Red Sea to East Africa

The Somali coast sat at the junction of three trade systems: the Red Sea, the Persian Gulf, and East Africa. Ships came when the monsoons allowed, returned when the winds reversed. For two thousand years, this rhythm dictated when merchants could depart, what cargo they carried, and where they could sell it.

The System

The Monsoon Calendar

Trade followed the wind. The southwest monsoon—called Xagaa in Somali—brought ships from India and the Persian Gulf from May through October. The northeast monsoon—the Jilaal—returned them homeward from November through April. No merchant could fight this rhythm. The ports themselves were timed to it.

Each port controlled different trade routes into the interior. Each specialized in a specific cargo.

The Cargo

What the Ports Traded

From Oponē, merchants exported cinnamon brought from interior highlands and the frankincense groves of what is now Dhofar. Myrrh came from Malao. Tortoiseshell, ambergris, and ivory flowed north to the Red Sea and south to the Indian Ocean. In return, ships brought Indian cotton from Cambay, Roman glassware, Egyptian grain, and coins3Casson, Periplus, pp. 79-81..

This was not incidental trade. The Periplus records that the traders at Oponē "are better men"—a phrase indicating their status and the importance of the port. Frankincense was not a luxury; it was a necessity in Rome, valued at times more than gold. When the supply dried, prices rose in the imperial capital. The merchants of Oponē understood exactly what they possessed.

The Medieval City

Ibn Battuta's Mogadishu, 1331

In 1331, the Moroccan traveler Ibn Battuta arrived in Mogadishu during the high trading season. He recorded a city where textiles were "unequalled," exported northward to Egypt and Syria in such quantity that entire neighborhoods produced cloth for foreign markets. The Sultan was Abu Bakr ibn Shaikh 'Umar, who received foreign merchants according to protocols that presume constant commerce. Battuta notes the presence of a scholar, Sa'i, who had traveled to India and China and could speak to the merchant class in their own languages4Ibn Battuta, trans. H.A.R. Gibb, The Travels of Ibn Battuta, A.D. 1325–1354, vol. 2 (Hakluyt Society, 1962), pp. 374-377..

The city was organized around exchange. Merchants did not visit for a few months and depart; they settled into quarters, married, built houses, and remained for years. Mogadishu's economy was not based on the export of raw materials extracted from the interior. It was a manufacturing center.

The Textile System

The Toob Benadir

By the nineteenth century, one in every five residents of Mogadishu was a weaver. The toob benadir—the cloth of the Benadir coast—was a specialized product. Between 1840 and 1860, Mogadishu, Merca, and Barawa exported between 360,000 and 380,000 pieces annually5Lee V. Cassanelli, The Shaping of Somali Society (University of Pennsylvania Press, 1982), pp. 125-131.. This was not incidental production. This was an industrial economy scaled to the logistics of ocean trade. The cloth was dyed to specific standards for Egyptian and Syrian markets. Designs changed based on demand signals sent back from Cairo. The weaving guilds of Mogadishu understood their customer base precisely because they lived at the intersection of two worlds: Indian Ocean traders and Somali interior merchants who carried goods to and from the Harar region.

The Chinese Connection

Duan Chengshi to Zheng He

The first Chinese written account of East Africa appears in the work of Duan Chengshi, a Tang Dynasty scholar who compiled the Youyang Zazu around 863 CE. He mentions "Bobali"—Berbera—as a trading center for ivory, ambergris, and enslaved persons6Duan Chengshi, Youyang Zazu, c. 863 CE, cited in J.J.L. Duyvendak, China's Discovery of Africa (Arthur Probsthain, 1949), pp. 13-15.. This was not accidental knowledge. The trade goods Duan lists match precisely what was being shipped north on merchant vessels: luxury items and human cargo bound for Arab and Persian markets.

Five and a half centuries later, the Ming admiral Zheng He reached Mogadishu and Brava on his fifth voyage (1417–1419) with a fleet so large that contemporary observers described the ships as cities. Ma Huan, the translator aboard the fleet, recorded the visit in his Yingya Shenglan7Ma Huan, Yingya Shenglan [Overall Survey of the Ocean's Shores], trans. J.V.G. Mills (Hakluyt Society, 1970), pp. 150-153. (1451). Fei Xin's Xingcha Shenglan (1436) corroborates the account. Both describe the ports as trading centers with established merchant communities and sophisticated harbor facilities.

Chinese ceramics found at Mogadishu archaeological sites span from the Tang Dynasty (618–907) through the Portuguese period, suggesting unbroken ceramic trade for nearly nine hundred years8Marie-Pierre Ballarin, "Chinese-Style Ceramics in East Africa from the 9th to the 16th Century," Afriques 5 (2014).. Tang Dynasty copper coins bearing the Kaiyuan reign mark have been excavated at Mogadishu, evidence that Chinese commerce reached the coast as a monetary economy, not barter.

The Portuguese Arrival

Duarte Barbosa and the Transformation

Around 1516–1518, the Portuguese merchant and official Duarte Barbosa compiled accounts from the Indian Ocean network. His observations of Mogadishu record "much gold, ivory, beeswax" being exported. He notes that ships regularly arrived from Cambay in Gujarat carrying "stuffs of all kinds and spices." He also records, with apparent indifference, that Brava was "already destroyed by the Portuguese, with great slaughter"9Duarte Barbosa, The Book of Duarte Barbosa, trans. M.L. Dames (Hakluyt Society, 1918), vol. 1, pp. 24-26..

Vasco da Gama's anonymous diarist, recording the first Portuguese expedition (1499), described Mogadishu as "a large city with houses of four or five storeys high." Within a generation, those houses were ruins10A Journal of the First Voyage of Vasco da Gama, 1497–1499, trans. E.G. Ravenstein (Hakluyt Society, 1898), pp. 48-49..

Navigation and Seamanship

Ahmad ibn Majid and the Science of Monsoons

Ahmad ibn Majid was born around 1432 in Julfar, on the coast of present-day Oman. He became the most renowned navigator of the Indian Ocean, serving sultans and recording his knowledge in the Kitab al-Fawa'id fi Usul 'Ilm al-Bahr wa'l-Qawa'id (Book of the Benefits of Principles and Foundations of Seamanship). This work systematizes what all the merchants and pilots knew through experience: the exact timing of monsoons, the location of hazards, the positions of ports from East Africa to Indonesia, and the seasonal winds that governed every voyage11Ahmad ibn Majid, Kitab al-Fawa'id, cited in G.R. Tibbetts, Arab Navigation in the Indian Ocean before the Coming of the Portuguese (Royal Asiatic Society, 1971), pp. 175-200..

Ibn Majid's text creates a unified system. Aden, Mogadishu, Kilwa, and Madagascar are not separate ports. They are nodes in a network linked by shared winds, shared currents, and shared commerce. A merchant who understood ibn Majid's system could navigate from the Somali coast to the Swahili coast to the Indian coast and back again, timing each voyage to arrive in the season when customers were ready to buy and winds were ready to carry ships home.
