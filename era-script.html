The Historical Context

The Problem

Somali was the language of 98% of the population. It had no standard written form. 1Laitin, David D. Politics, Language, and Thought: The Somali Experience (University of Chicago Press, 1977), pp. 1–18. Colonial administrators in Italian, British, and French territories wrote in Italian, English, and French. Court proceedings, government documents, contracts, treaties — all conducted in the colonizer's script. The legal system depended on written records Somali speakers could not read.

The oral tradition was the complete cultural infrastructure. Every legal claim lived in the gabay, the memorized recitation, the witness who spoke before a council. A Somali marriage contract had no written enforcement. A land boundary had no written deed. A trade agreement was memory and witness.

But a modern state required written law, printed textbooks, civil records, and bureaucracy. The British had begun printing in Somali using Latin letters. The Italians had experimented with Arabic script adapted to Somali sounds. Independent Somalia would not inherit a colonial language authority — it had to choose.

The First Proposal · 1920s

Osman Yusuf Kenadid and the Osmanya Script

Osman Yusuf Kenadid was the son of Sultan Yusuf Ali Kenadid of the Hobyo Sultanate on the northern coast. He belonged to the Majeerteen clan, the dominant power in that region. In the 1920s, he did something unprecedented: he invented an alphabet specifically for Somali. 2Laitin, Politics, Language, and Thought, pp. 82–95.

The Osmanya script was phonetically sophisticated. It bore no resemblance to Arabic or Latin. It used curved and angular lines in a novel arrangement, 22 characters covering every Somali phoneme with clean separation. 3Tosco, Mauro. "The Case of Cushitic Languages," in The Oxford Handbook of the History of Linguistics, ed. Keith Allan (Oxford University Press, 2013), pp. 271–290. He introduced it into local schools. The script gained acceptance for correspondence, bookkeeping, and commercial record-keeping in the Hobyo region.

Then the Italian colonial authorities imprisoned him in Mogadishu. They recognized what the script represented: nationalist sentiment, cultural autonomy, a claim to Somali identity that did not require colonial mediation. All script development halted for 25 years. 4Laitin, Politics, Language, and Thought, pp. 85–90. Osmanya survived in diaspora use and manuscript copying, but lost institutional momentum.

Competing Scripts · 1930s–1950s

The Borama, Kaddare, and Arabic Claims

In 1933, Sheikh Abdurahman Sheikh Nuur of the Gadabuursi clan invented the Borama script. 5Laitin, Politics, Language, and Thought, pp. 95–112. It was used mainly around Borama in the northwest, and gained a small body of qasida poetry written in it. The script was functional but regionally limited.

In 1952, Hussein Sheikh Ahmed Kaddare of the Abgaal Hawiye clan invented the Kaddare script. Technical commissions found it "very accurate" for representing Somali phonemes. 6Andrzejewski, B.W. "The Development of a National Orthography in Somalia and the Modernization of the Somali Language," Horn of Africa 1, no. 3 (1978), pp. 39–45. Yet it never gained wider use beyond manuscript study.

Arabic script — Wadaad's Writing — had been used for Islamic education for centuries. Religious conservatives advocated for it as the official script. But Arabic script cannot represent all Somali phonemes without diacritical marks. The practical limitation was significant: the pharyngeal consonants in Somali, the ejectives, the interdental sounds — Arabic could only approximate them. 7Laitin, Politics, Language, and Thought, pp. 95–112.

Each script carried a clan identifier. Osmanya meant Majeerteen. Borama meant Gadabuursi. Kaddare meant Hawiye. Arabic meant religious authority, the Wadaads, the Muslim scholars. This was the political problem. In a nation where clan identity determined land rights, trade access, political alliance, and military loyalty, the question "which alphabet?" was inseparable from the question "which clan gets to name the nation?"

"The choice of script was not a linguistic matter at all. It was a decision about power."

— Summary of Laitin, Politics, Language, and Thought, pp. 125–142
The Decision · October 1972

Latin Wins — Why

On October 21, 1972, Siad Barre's military government issued a decree. The Somali language would become the sole official language of the state, written in Latin characters using the orthography designed by Shire Jama Ahmed. 8Shire Jama Ahmed, cited in Laitin, Politics, Language, and Thought, pp. 115–125. The government submitted 18 competing scripts to a Language Committee. Latin was chosen.

Shire Jama's argument was pragmatic. Printing presses worldwide were set to Latin. Typewriters already in the country used Latin keyboards. Latin provided "simplicity, ability to cope with all sounds in the language, and compatibility with existing infrastructure." 9Shire Jama Ahmed, cited in Laitin, Politics, Language, and Thought, pp. 115–125. The practical case was unassailable.

But the political case went deeper. Five reasons converged on Latin:

Machine compatibility. The existing infrastructure — typewriters, printing presses, telegraph equipment — was set to Latin. Changing to Osmanya or Borama would have required replacing every machine.

Clan neutrality. Osmanya was Majeerteen. Arabic was religious-conservative. Latin was nobody's and therefore everybody's. It carried no historical claim by any Somali clan. 10Laitin, Politics, Language, and Thought, pp. 125–142.

Cold War alignment. Siad Barre's regime was Soviet-backed. The USSR had conducted massive literacy campaigns in Cuba, Tanzania, and South Yemen using Latin characters. The Soviets understood Latin infrastructure. Barre framed Latin adoption as part of a modernization program aligned with socialist literacy campaigns in the developing world. 11Kapteijns, Lidwien. "Making Memories: The Construction of Somali National Culture through Radio Mogadishu in the 1950s–1960s," Journal of African Cultural Studies 14, no. 1 (2001), pp. 83–94.

Speed. Latin was the fastest path to mass literacy. Textbooks could be printed immediately. Teachers trained in weeks. The campaigns could begin within months.

Anti-clan politics. Barre's regime was actively burning effigies of tribalism — qabyaalad — in all 13 quarters of Mogadishu. Choosing any indigenous script would have been choosing a clan. Choosing Osmanya meant favoring Majeerteen. Choosing Borama meant favoring Gadabuursi. Choosing Kaddare meant favoring Hawiye. Latin meant neutrality. 12Laitin, Politics, Language, and Thought, pp. 125–142.

The Somalization Campaign

Writing the Nation in Three Months

Within three months of October 1972, the entire state apparatus switched to Somali in Latin script. All government communications, all bureaucratic forms, all official records were "Somalized." 13Samatar, Ahmed Ismail. Socialist Somalia: Rhetoric and Reality (Zed Books, 1988), pp. 95–108. Civil servants who could not write Somali Latin were sent to literacy classes. Documentation systems were rebuilt. The practical transition was faster than anyone had predicted.

Then came the rural campaigns. Students and civil servants were deployed to nomadic communities, pastoralist areas, farming regions. They taught reading and writing using poetry. The gabay — the oral verse form that had carried Somali culture for centuries — became the vehicle for written literacy. 14Samatar, Socialist Somalia: Rhetoric and Reality, pp. 95–108.

Radio Mogadishu broadcast newly composed gabay in Somali Latin script. Young people memorized verses, then learned to write them down. The oral tradition became the bridge to written culture. Literacy rates climbed from under 5% to over 24% in a decade. 15Kapteijns, Lidwien. "Discourse on Moral Womanhood in Somali Popular Songs, 1960–1990," Journal of African History 50, no. 1 (2009), pp. 101–122.

"The campaigns used poetry as the delivery mechanism. The form that had kept the language alive in oral tradition now carried it into written culture."

— From literacy campaign records, 1972–1982
The Aftermath · 1972–Present

What Was Lost and What Survived

The Osmanya script survived in diaspora communities. As Somali refugees fled the regime's collapse in the 1980s and 1990s, they carried Osmanya manuscripts with them. In the 2000s, Osmanya was encoded in Unicode — the digital character set that allows any script to be typed and displayed. 16Tosco, The Case of Cushitic Languages, pp. 280–285. It appears on websites, in diaspora publications, in cultural preservation projects.

But the living use died. Children who learned to read in the 1970s learned Latin. Children born after 1975 had no exposure to Osmanya as a functional writing system. The Borama and Kaddare scripts exist now only in academic papers and archived manuscripts. No one writes new literature in them.

The gabay survived the transition completely. Somali poetry continued to be composed and performed, now increasingly in written form as well as oral. The verse forms that had carried legal claims, love stories, political arguments, and historical narratives in the pre-literate era continued to carry them after 1972. The alphabetic change did not break the poetic chain.

The question of whether a non-Latin script would have changed Somalia's relationship to its own literature remains unresolved. Would Osmanya have carried different cultural weight? Would the visual form of the alphabet have mattered? Would a Majeerteen script have provoked a different clan response? These are not answerable questions. History did not run that experiment. 17Tosco, The Case of Cushitic Languages, pp. 280–285. What is clear is that the script debates did not survive the decision. Within a generation, Latin was assumed as inevitable. The competing claims of Osmanya, Borama, Kaddare, and Arabic faded into historical footnotes.
