THE ARCHIVE

What Is Xog

Somali — English The Word

xog (f.n.) — secret; the essential nature of an issue; that which cannot be ignored. From the Afmaal Somali-English Dictionary. The secretary is the xoghaye. The keeper of the essential record. That is what this archive does.

The Mission

This archive exists so any Somali person—wherever they are—can find a pillar to lean on when they need to ground their identity. Not nationalism. Infrastructure.

The difference matters. Nationalism tells you what to feel. Infrastructure gives you what you need to stand on your own ground.

When you know what Ibn Battuta saw in Mogadishu, when you read what the British officer misunderstood about the Sayyid, when you hold the Somali counter-narrative in your hand—you are not dependent on someone else's story about who you are. You have the materials to build it yourself.

The Method

Every claim in this archive is defensible because the source is present. If something comes from an oral tradition, the archive says so. It does not dress oral knowledge in academic formality to make it seem more "objective." Oral knowledge is exact knowledge—it has been refined through centuries of recitation. It travels because it must survive without paper.

When two sources contradict, both are here. The Sayyid was a poet of extraordinary power and a military commander who brought devastation. Both are true. The xeer created the stability that allowed the absence of a state and fractured the possibility of a state. Both are true. These contradictions are not weaknesses. They are the archive's honesty.

Provenance shapes meaning. Who recorded something, when, for what audience—these details are load-bearing. The archive travels with them. This is not neutrality. This is rigor.

What the Archive Contains

Fourteen historical eras. Ten source documents. Footnotes throughout so every claim can be tested. Oral poetry preserved not as artifact but as argument. Colonial records read against themselves. Counter-narratives that center the Somali reading of history.

The archive does not pretend to completeness. A comprehensive history of the Horn does not fit here. Instead, the archive is built on the principle of the pillar—sturdy foundations that someone standing on them can reach further than they could alone.

The Contradictions Stay In

A history that only celebrates is propaganda. The archive holds uncomfortable truths because they are part of the ground you stand on. The clan system that made the xeer possible—that allowed resource distribution and conflict resolution to persist without state machinery—was the same system that fragmented the possibility of a unified republic. Both truths occupy the same moment. Both demand recognition.

The Sayyid wrote poetry of stunning precision and composed verses that moved those who heard them to action and to sacrifice. He also waged war that cost lives and displaced communities. To know him, you must hold both. To revise history by removing either one is to falsify it in the service of feeling better about the past.

This archive refuses that comfort. It offers something harder and more useful: the materials to think clearly about who you come from, and therefore who you are capable of becoming.
