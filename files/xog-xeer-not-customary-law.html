The Xeer Is Not “Customary Law”

COUNTER-NARRATIVE · LEGAL HISTORY · SOMALI LEGAL TRADITION

Western anthropologists classified the xeer as "customary law" — a category that implies static tradition, pre-modern simplicity, and an inevitable replacement by written legal codes. The xeer is none of these things. It is a decentralised legal system with built-in renegotiability, a compensation calculus more mathematically precise than most tort systems, and a 1,400-year operational history governing populations across 700,000 square kilometres.

The xeer is not written. This is treated as a deficiency in the Western legal tradition, where writing confers legitimacy. In the xeer system, the absence of writing is a feature: it ensures that law remains a living negotiation between parties rather than a fixed text subject to institutional capture. Any ruling can be reopened if circumstances change. The law evolves continuously without legislation.

The xeerbeeg — the jurist — does not argue. The word means "one who measures the law." The xeeryaqaan — the lawyer — "knows the law." The vocabulary itself describes a system of measurement and knowledge, not argumentation and persuasion.

The diya (compensation) tables are the system’s operating core. They assign specific values to every category of harm: death, injury by body part, insult, property damage, breach of contract. The values are negotiated between diya-paying groups and updated as circumstances change. The system predates and postdates every state that has governed Somali territory.

The correction is not that xeer is better than Western law. The correction is that it is not the primitive precursor the label "customary law" implies. It is a different kind of legal technology.
