The Sky Was a Calendar

Somali pastoralists ran an economy on stars. The moon's path was divided into 28 lodgings, the Pleiades timed the lambing, Spica named half the year, and the experts who kept this knowledge, the xiddigiye, were consulted before journeys, matings, and war. None of it was written down. All of it was exact.

God 1
Station of the moon · Jiilaal quarter

The moon lodges in one of 28 stations along its path, each marked by fixed stars. The Somali word is god: a den, a dug place, where the moon sleeps for a night. Reading which station holds the moon, and which holds it at dusk against the season, was the working method of Somali weather prediction.

Step the moon through its month. The phase waxes as it walks.

The Xiddigiye

Every camp had access to a man who read the sky for a living. Galaal, who spent years collecting this knowledge from the nomadic experts before it thinned, records that the Somali peninsula was divided into seven weather districts, each with its own reading tradition and its own names for the stations. The xiddigiye advised when to move herds, when to expect the rains to break, which season's sky a newborn arrived under. His instrument was memory; his observatory was every clear night of the year.

Urur, and the Night Called Dambasamo

The Pleiades cluster is Urur, the confederation, also called Laxo, the sheep. In the middle of autumn, on the night the full moon stands in conjunction with Urur, the rams were loosed among the ewes. That night has its own name: Dambasamo. Lambs conceived then arrive about 150 days later, in the middle of the Gu rains, when there is milk and grass. By then Urur is setting at twilight, and its setting announced the rains themselves.

One conjunction, observed once a year, scheduled the reproduction of the national herd.

Dirir, Who Names Half the Year

The star Western astronomy calls Spica is Dirir. The second half of the Somali year runs under its name, the Bilo Dirir, the months of Dirir, counted by the moon's conjunctions with it. Where the solar reckoning of seasons and the lunar reckoning of months needed to interlock, Dirir was the pin.

The Four Quarters

The year turns through Gu, the long rains; Xagaa, the dry monsoon summer; Dayr, the short rains; and Jiilaal, the hard dry winter. The seasons are solar, the months are lunar, and the station system is what held the two calendars together, accurate enough that researchers who later compared its predictions against meteorological records found a working forecasting system, not folklore.

Navigation, Sea and Land

The same sky steered movement. Dhows ran the monsoon coast with the wind at their backs and the stars overhead; camel caravans crossed featureless plains at night to spare the animals, steering by the fixed points of the sky. The map page of this archive and the sky wheel above are drawn from the same conviction: Somalis navigated by what they knew, and what they knew was precise.
