The Sufi Brotherhoods

Schools · Settlements · Parallel Authority

Three Sufi orders — the Qadiriyya, Ahmadiyya, and Salihiyya — built a parallel infrastructure across Somalia. They established schools, agricultural settlements, and mediation systems that operated alongside clan authority. The orders were not merely spiritual: they became economic institutions, landowners, judges, and rival powers to the clan elders themselves.

The Islamic Brotherhoods

The Three Orders

The Qadiriyya arrived in Somalia in 1503 through Sheikh Abu Bakr al-Aydarus, whose teachings created the earliest Sufi communities.1I.M. Lewis, Saints and Somalis: Popular Islam in a Clan-Based Society (Red Sea Press, 1998), pp. 8–15Detailed account of Qadiri establishment and theological foundations The order was already centuries old — Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani had founded it in Baghdad in the twelfth century — but its spread to the Horn of Africa was slow and deliberate. The Qadiriyya taught tawassul, intercession through saints, and attracted followers by promising spiritual connection to the Prophet through living masters.

The Ahmadiyya arrived later, introduced by Ali Maye Durogba of Merca. This order traced its authority to Ahmad ibn Idris al-Fasi (1760–1837), a Moroccan scholar who created a reformist branch of Sufism. The Ahmadiyya was more austere than the Qadiriyya, rejecting some of the ecstatic practices of other orders while maintaining mystical devotion.2B.G. Martin, Muslim Brotherhoods in Nineteenth-Century Africa (Cambridge University Press, 1976), pp. 152–176Comprehensive treatment of Ahmadi theology and regional spread

The Salihiyya, founded by Muhammad Salih (1854–1919) in Sudan, reached Somalia through multiple propagators and became the most controversial. The Salihiyya was explicitly reformist, rejecting bid'ah (religious innovation) and tawassul as incompatible with strict monotheism. In Somalia, the most prominent Salihiyya adherent was Muhammad Abdullah Hassan — the man history would call the Mad Mullah.3Abdi Sheik-Abdi, Divine Madness: Mohammed Abdulle Hassan (1856–1920) (Zed Books, 1993), pp. 45–62Contextualizes Hassan's Salihiyya allegiance within broader Islamic reform movements

"The Islamic brotherhoods provided an organizational framework that transcended clan divisions. They offered spiritual authority backed by written Quranic knowledge — a form of legitimacy unavailable to secular clan leaders."

Sheikh Uways al-Barawi

1847–1909: The Uwaysiyya Founder

Sheikh Uways al-Barawi was born in Brava in 1847. He became the most influential figure of the early Qadiriyya in Somalia. After studying Islamic law in the Hijaz and Egypt, he returned to the Horn of Africa and established a new branch of the Qadiriyya called the Uwaysiyya. Unlike the order as it existed elsewhere, the Uwaysiyya emphasized settlement and community-building over peripatetic mysticism.

Uways established settled communities called jamaa'a — literally "assemblies" or "communities" — throughout southern Somalia. These were not monasteries but agricultural villages where Sufi followers lived permanently, practicing Islam through work and study. The jamaa'a were his innovation, his contribution to Somali Islam. By the time of his death, settlements bearing his influence extended beyond Somalia into Mozambique and Madagascar.4François Bontinck, "Sheikh Uways b. Muhammad al-Barawi et la confrérie Qadiriyya en Afrique orientale," Transafrican Journal of History 5, no. 2 (1976), pp. 63–82Archival treatment of Uways's regional influence and organizational legacy

His assassination in 1909 was a watershed. Rival Salihiyya followers killed him — a political act justified through theological language. After his death, Muhammad Abdullah Hassan composed a triumphant gabay celebrating the removal of a dangerous innovator. But in southern Somalia, the Uwaysiyya settlements continued. Uways became a saint whose tomb at Biyoley became a pilgrimage site, a center of spiritual authority that would persist for over a century.

The Jamaa'a Settlements

Communities of Law and Land

A Sufi leader would approach a lineage head and request permission to settle land. The word used was "freely given" — but the geography was significant. Settlements were often positioned between two hostile clans, serving as buffer zones where neither group could fully dominate. They offered a neutral ground for trade and a refuge from clan violence.

By the 1950s, Sufi settlements numbered over 90 throughout southern Somalia, with approximately 35,000 residents. Most clustered along the Jubba and Shebelle river valleys, where water and arable land made agricultural life sustainable.5Lee V. Cassanelli, The Shaping of Somali Society: Reconstructing the History of a Pastoral People, 1600–1900 (University of Pennsylvania Press, 1982), pp. 187–196Demographic and spatial analysis of settlement patterns The settlements were economically productive: they cleared brush to reduce tsetse fly habitat, making surrounding pasturelands more usable. They established grain cultivation in regions previously dependent on herding.

What made them distinct was their dual function: school and farm. The settlement taught Quranic recitation, Arabic language, and Islamic law. It also organized cooperative cultivation and harvesting. Children learned to read the Quran while working in fields. The integration of spiritual and economic life was the genius of the jamaa'a model. Families joined because they received both religious education and economic security.

The settlements also became centers of Islamic justice. Sufi leaders served as judges for disputes brought before them. A clan elder might refer a dispute to the settlement's sheikh if the matter involved a settlement member or if the settlement's intervention was seen as more legitimate than clan adjudication. In some cases, the settlements became rival authorities to the traditional xeer system.6Virginia Luling, "The Social Structure of Southern Somali Tribes" (PhD diss., University of London, 1971), pp. 183–198Ethnographic documentation of settlement governance structures

Theological Split

The Salihiyya and the Rejection of Intercession

The core theological division was not minor. The Qadiriyya practiced tawassul — seeking intercession through saints. The Salihiyya explicitly rejected this as bid'ah (religious innovation) and shirk (associating partners with God). The argument was subtle in theological terms but consequential in practice: the Salihiyya claimed that the Qadiriyya were corrupting monotheism by elevating saints to intercessory status.

Muhammad Salih in Sudan had created a consciously reformist movement. His followers believed Islam had been contaminated by local superstition and saint veneration. In Somalia, this theological position aligned perfectly with the political interests of warriors like Muhammad Abdullah Hassan. Declaring takfir (religious judgment) against the Qadiriyya and Sheikh Uways meant more than doctrinal correction — it meant delegitimizing a rival power structure.

The theological fault line mapped directly onto geography. The Qadiriyya settlements were concentrated in river regions where they had established land claims. The Salihiyya gained traction among pastoral clans in the interior and among the military followers of the Sayyid. In many places, the orders did not coexist peacefully. They competed for followers, for resources, for spiritual authority. The competition was real and sometimes violent.7I.M. Lewis, A Modern History of the Somali: Nation and State in the Horn of Africa, 4th ed. (Ohio University Press, 2002), pp. 63–72Political and theological dimensions of the Qadiri-Salihiyya divide

Poetry as Warfare

The Sayyid and Sheikh Uways Exchange Verses

In Somali culture, poetry is a weapon. Muhammad Abdullah Hassan and Sheikh Uways did not wage physical war on each other — they waged poetic war. The Sayyid composed gabay attacking Uways for innovation and idolatry. Uways replied with verses accusing the Sayyid of murder dressed in religious language. The poetry circulated orally, memorized and transmitted by those who supported each side.

After Uways's assassination in 1909, the Sayyid composed a triumphant poem celebrating his death. The oral record preserves both the Sayyid's attack and the Qadiri response. These poems are not mere rhetoric — they are theological arguments compressed into meter and transmitted through generations. They shaped how Somalis understood the difference between the orders. To this day, scholars can quote the verses.

The poetry war was also a war over legitimacy. Who was the true defender of Islam? The Sayyid claimed it was those who rejected innovation. The Qadiriyya claimed it was those who maintained the mystical path. Neither side was marginal; both had followers, resources, and influence. The conflict was not resolved by theological victory but by historical events — the collapse of the Sayyid's state, the persistence of Qadiri communities despite his opposition.8Said S. Samatar, Oral Poetry and Somali Nationalism: The Case of Sayyid Mahammad 'Abdille Hasan (Cambridge University Press, 1982), pp. 124–135Detailed analysis of poetry exchanges and their political meaning

The Northern Reformer

Sheikh Madar and the Founding of Hargeisa

In the north, a Qadiriyya leader named Sheikh Madar Ahmed Shirwa (1825–1918) established settlements that transformed the landscape. Sheikh Madar founded communities at Hargeisa, Gibeley, and Borama. These settlements drew nomadic pastoralists into sedentary life. The settlement at Hargeisa became the seed from which the modern city grew.9Mohamed Haji Ingiriis, The Suicidal State in Somalia: The Rise and Fall of the Siad Barre Regime, 1969–1991 (University Press of America, 2016), pp. 22–25Urban origins and settlement-to-city transformation

The conversion of pastoral populations into settled agricultural and trading communities through Sufi leadership was not accidental. The leaders understood it as religious obligation — to teach Islam through example, to create communities where Islamic law could be practiced, to offer an alternative to the perpetual insecurity of pastoral raiding.

Modern Reckoning

Legacy and Destruction

The three orders persisted through colonialism. When the Somali state attempted to modernize Islam in the twentieth century, they absorbed the theological critique of bid'ah, but they did not eliminate the orders. The orders remained embedded in the communities they had founded. The settlements continued. The tombs of saints remained pilgrimage sites.

In 1991, when the Somali state collapsed, the three orders united. The Qadiriyya, Ahmadiyya, and Salihiyya formed a single coalition called Ahlu Sunna Wal Jamaa to resist al-Shabaab. The enemy was not each other but the Wahhabi-influenced ideology of a new generation of Islamists. For the first time in over a century, the theological divisions that had separated the orders seemed less important than their common interest in defending Somali Islam against external threat.

But that defense came with a cost. Al-Shabaab destroyed the tombs of saints throughout southern Somalia. The tomb of Sheikh Uways at Biyoley was destroyed in 2009. The destruction of the tomb was the destruction of accumulated spiritual authority — a century of pilgrimage, prayer, and devotion deliberately erased. Other tombs followed. The Wahhabi critique of saint veneration, which the Salihiyya had voiced in theological language, was now being enacted in violence.10Stig Jarle Hansen, Al-Shabaab in Somalia: The History and Ideology of a Militant Islamist Group (Oxford University Press, 2013), pp. 52–58Documentation of shrine destruction and its meaning within Islamist ideology

Today, the settlements have largely emptied. Some persist as communities, but they no longer function as they did in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The jamaa'a model — combining Islamic education with agricultural production and alternative authority structures — has largely been displaced by modern state education and capitalist agriculture. Yet the legacy remains in the toponymy, in oral memory, and in the surviving tombs that have not been destroyed.

Northern Settler
1825–1918. Qadiriyya leader. Founded settlements at Hargeisa, Gibeley, and Borama. His work transformed pastoralist regions into agricultural communities and urban centers.

Ahmadi Propagator
Based in Merca. Primary figure in establishing Ahmadiyya order in Somalia. Brought reformist branch of Sufism to Horn of Africa with emphasis on austerity and Islamic authenticity.
