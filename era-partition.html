The Partition
of Somali Territories

1884 – 1960 · Five Colonial Powers, One People

In the decades of the Scramble for Africa, five European and neighboring powers partitioned Somali territory without consulting any Somali leader. The Ogaden was given to Ethiopia. British and Italian Somaliland were created as separate colonies. France took the port of Djibouti. Kenya took the Northern Frontier District. The borders drawn at conference tables in Berlin and London would shape Somali politics for a century and catalyze every major conflict of the post-independence era.

The Division

Five Powers, One Territory

Between 1880 and 1900, Somali territory was divided among five different colonial administrations.1I.M. Lewis, A Modern History of the Somali, 4th ed. (Ohio University Press, 2002), pp. 62-85.

British Somaliland controlled the coast from Zeila to Kismayo. Italian Somalia controlled the central and southern regions, including Mogadishu and the Shabelle-Jubba river valleys. French Somaliland was limited to the strategic port city of Djibouti and its immediate hinterland. Ethiopia, which had never been colonized, had the Ogaden region granted to it through treaties with Britain and Italy that Somali clans never signed. Kenya controlled the Northern Frontier District, grazing lands used by Somali pastoral clans.

The borders were arbitrary, following no ethnic boundaries, no geographical logic, no political precedent.2Robert L. Hess, Italian Colonialism in Somalia (University of Chicago Press, 1966), pp. 45-78. A Somali clan whose pastoral territory crossed from British to Italian administration would find itself split between two different colonial tax systems. The port of Mogadishu, which had been a unified commercial center under the Ajuran Sultanate and later Adal, became a capital of an Italian colony with a hinterland controlled through different administrative systems.

This partition was not the result of Somali request or negotiation. No Somali representatives attended the Berlin Conference of 1884 that established the rules for the partition.3Cedric Barnes, "U dhashay — Ku dhashay: Genealogical and Territorial Discourse in Somali History," Social Identities 12, no. 4 (2006), pp. 487-498. Britain and Italy divided their spheres of influence in Somalia between themselves. Ethiopia under Emperor Menelik II used treaty negotiations with Britain and Italy to extend Ethiopian control into the Ogaden. France took Djibouti because of its strategic position on the Red Sea. The Somali clans that inhabited these territories did not consent to these arrangements.

Colonial Administration

Different Systems, Same Exploitation

The five colonial powers administered their territories according to different philosophies and with different intensities. British Somaliland used indirect rule, governing through existing clan structures and leaving pastoral society relatively undisturbed. Italian Somalia attempted more direct administration and settler colonialism, especially in the southern agricultural regions. The Italians established plantations, imported settlers, and attempted to build infrastructure more aggressively than the British.

French Djibouti was essentially a port city with a French garrison and French commercial interests. It was not a territory so much as a colonial foothold.

Ethiopia administered the Ogaden as tributary territory, extracting taxes and tribute from Somali pastoralists while allowing traditional clan governance to continue. Emperor Menelik II, who had decisively defeated Italy at the Battle of Adwa in 1896, was careful to establish Ethiopian sovereignty without attempting the level of direct administration the Italians pursued in Italian Somaliland.

What united all five colonial administrations was the extraction of resources and the subordination of Somali interests to external power. Pastoral lands were taxed. Trade was monopolized. Local governance structures were undermined. The pastoral system that had sustained Somali societies for centuries was disrupted by colonial policies designed to extract revenue.5Said S. Samatar, Oral Poetry and Somali Nationalism: The Case of Sayyid Maḥammad ʻAbdille Ḥasan (Cambridge University Press, 1982), pp. 143–167. Samatar examines how colonial taxation and administrative control disrupted pastoral economies and generated Somali resistance movements. British and Italian administrations competed for the same pastoral tax base. Ethiopian rule in the Ogaden created tension and resistance.

The Ogaden Betrayal

How Ethiopia Acquired Somali Territory

The clearest example of Somali dispossession without Somali consent was the Ogaden. The Ogaden is inhabited by Somali pastoralists — the Ogaden clan and related groups. But in 1897, the Anglo-Ethiopian Treaty granted the Ogaden to Ethiopia.4I.M. Lewis, A Modern History of the Somali, 4th ed. (Ohio University Press, 2002), pp. 83-88. This was not the result of Ethiopian conquest. It was a treaty signed between Britain and Ethiopia, in which Britain, which had no direct authority over the Ogaden, agreed to recognize Ethiopian sovereignty over it.

The Somali Ogaden clans were not parties to this negotiation. They did not cede their territory. They did not agree to Ethiopian rule. The treaty was a transaction between two outside powers in which Somali land was treated as a commodity to be traded. It established a precedent that would shape Somali politics for a century: that external powers could dispose of Somali territory without consulting the Somali people who inhabited it.

Ethiopian administration of the Ogaden created lasting resentment. The Somali clans resisted Ethiopian tax collection. The Ethiopian government, particularly under Haile Selassie in the 20th century, responded with military pressure. The Ogaden, which should have been part of a unified Somali state, instead became a flashpoint of tension between Somalia and Ethiopia — a dispute that would not be resolved until the 1977–78 Ogaden War, nearly a century after the initial partition.

The same dynamic applied to Kenya's Northern Frontier District. The NFD was inhabited by Somali pastoral clans, but after independence, Kenya refused to allow it to join Somalia. The result was a border dispute that lasted decades.

The Road to Independence

1960 and the Unification Dream

After World War II, the wave of decolonization swept Africa. In 1960, British Somaliland and Italian Somaliland achieved independence on the same date — July 1 — and immediately merged into the Somali Republic. The dream of a unified Somali nation seemed suddenly possible.

But the partition's legacy remained. French Somaliland (now Djibouti) remained a French colony and did not join. The Ogaden remained Ethiopian territory. The Northern Frontier District remained part of Kenya. The Somali people found themselves divided among five different states, with only two unified at independence. This incompleteness would define Somali politics for decades.

The first government of independent Somalia was built on the premise that Somali territory should be unified — that the partition was illegitimate and should be reversed. This aspiration, known as Greater Somalia, drove Somali foreign policy until the late 20th century.6E.R. Turton, "Somali Resistance to Colonial Rule and the Development of Somali Political Activity in Kenya, 1884–1948," Journal of African History 13, no. 3 (1972), pp. 477–497. Turton documents Somali resistance to partition and the nationalist movements that emerged across colonial boundaries. It motivated wars with Ethiopia and Kenya. It shaped the Pan-Somali nationalism that united the 1960 independence movement. And it made the partition not merely a historical event but an ongoing political crisis.

Key Figures

Emperor Menelik II
Ethiopian Ruler
Defeated Italy at Adwa in 1896, establishing Ethiopia's independence. Negotiated treaties with Britain and Italy that granted him control of the Ogaden. Expanded Ethiopian territory while European powers were distracted.
Lord Salisbury
British Prime Minister
Oversaw British imperial expansion in East Africa. Negotiated the treaties that partitioned Somali territory between British and Italian spheres. Never consulted Somali leaders.
Haile Selassie
Later Ethiopian Emperor
Consolidated Ethiopian rule in the Ogaden during the 20th century. His administration enforced Ethiopian sovereignty in Somali territories, creating lasting resentment among Ogaden clans.
